My Obsidian Second Brain Setup in 2026

TS
By Tech Simpld
•
May 26, 2026
•
12 Min Read

I've been there too many times: deep into a project, knowing I read a brilliant article last week that perfectly solves my current problem, but I simply cannot find it. My bookmarks were a graveyard of unnamed links. My browser tabs were out of control. My Apple Notes app was a nightmare of grocery lists mixed with critical server configuration commands. Thirty minutes into searching for that one specific command, the flow state was entirely gone, and I would end up just re-Googling the problem from scratch.

It took me a long time to realize that your biological brain is engineered to generate ideas, not to act as a filing cabinet for a thousand random facts. That's exactly why I decided to build a Second Brain in Obsidian. It's a local-first, blazing-fast web of notes that handles the storage and retrieval process so I can focus entirely on the execution side of my projects. There are no expensive monthly SaaS subscriptions to pay, no cloud latency when my internet goes down, and no lock-in. It's just plain text Markdown files that link together at the speed of thought.

In this guide, I'm going to pull back the curtain and show you exactly how I operate my daily workflow using the Tech Simpld Obsidian Starter Vault. I will break down precisely how my daily logs connect to my long-term project tracking, how I handle meeting notes, and how automated indexes do all the heavy lifting so I never have to organize folders manually ever again.

Prerequisites: Setting Up Your Obsidian Environment

Before we dive into the actual daily workflows and how the data moves, you need a structured vault to work in. If you're starting entirely from scratch, I highly recommend stepping back and reading my Obsidian Starter Vault setup guide first.

That guide will walk you through the core folder structure you need, how to install the necessary community plugins, and how to apply the custom CSS themes I use. Most importantly, ensure you have the Dataview plugin installed and enabled, as we are going to rely heavily on it for the automation pieces of this setup. Without Dataview, a lot of the magic I'm going to show you simply won't work, and you'll be stuck manually updating lists of links.

Step 1: Daily Logs (The Command Center)

The daily note is the absolute engine of my entire productivity system. For a long time, I treated my daily note as a messy scratchpad where I'd just dump random thoughts and delete them the next day. That was a mistake. Now, I use it as a strict command center for my life. Every single morning, before I even look at my email or check my Proxmox cluster status, I open Obsidian, hit my hotkey for the Daily Note Template, and map out the next 12 hours of my day.

My daily template is intentionally rigid and is split into four strict sections to keep me on track:

  • Today's Focus (Rule of Three): I limit myself to exactly three major outcomes. If I nail these three tasks, I consider the day a massive win, regardless of what else catches on fire. Cramming twenty tasks here guarantees failure and burnout. This forces me to ruthlessly prioritize what actually matters.
  • Active Minor Tasks: This is for the small chores. "Email the accountant back," "fix the DNS record on Cloudflare," or "order more 3D printer filament." I keep these down here so they don't clutter the main focus area, but I still have a place to check them off as I get small breaks in the day.
  • Activity Journal: This section stays open on my second monitor all day long. Did I just finish a client call? I write a quick bullet point summarizing it. Did I find a weird bug in a Docker compose file? I paste the error message here. It acts as a breadcrumb trail for my day and keeps me from getting derailed by having to open new files or figure out where to put random pieces of information.
  • Daily Review: Before I shut down my workstation for the night, I spend exactly two minutes looking over the note. I write down what got blocked, why it got blocked, and what the priority is for tomorrow. It stops me from waking up overwhelmed the next day.

Step 2: Project Tracking & Managing Milestones

One of the biggest mistakes people make in productivity systems is confusing tasks with projects. A task is "update the firewall rules on the router." A project is "migrate the entire homelab routing infrastructure to OPNsense." For anything that is going to take more than a single day to complete, I deploy my Project Note Template.

I keep all of my active projects in a central Projects folder. Instead of scattering my research, links, and code snippets across fifty different files, the Project Note serves as the central hub. Every time I work on the project, I start from this note. I structure it exactly like this to keep things clear:

  1. Objective Statement: This is a single sentence defining what success looks like for the project. If I can't articulate it simply and clearly, it means the project isn't scoped correctly and is going to drag on forever.
  2. Milestones & Phases: I split the work into three distinct phases: Planning, Execution, and Review. Checking off these phases builds serious momentum. If a project has 50 tasks, looking at the list is demoralizing. If it has three phases, it feels manageable.
  3. Linked Assets: I deliberately do not dump all my research, PDFs, and code directly into the main project note. It makes the note too long and unreadable. Instead, I use internal Obsidian wikilinks like [[Proxmox Network Config]]. It keeps the main page clean while keeping the deep-dive technical details just one click away.

Step 3: Connecting Ideas with Obsidian Backlinks

Here is where Obsidian completely outshines traditional note-taking apps like Evernote or Notion. Folders are rigid and frustrating. Think about it: where does a note about a new Python API framework go? Does it go in the "Work" folder? The "Tech" folder? The "Ideas" folder? I don't care anymore, because I have completely stopped relying on folders for organization. Instead, I rely entirely on backlinking.

Let's say I'm building a new software tool called Project Alpha. While I'm writing in my daily journal on a random Tuesday, I might type something like:

"I spent three hours struggling with the backend authentication endpoint today. I had to reference the [[Project Alpha API Specs]] document extensively to figure out the token payload structure."

By wrapping the document name in double brackets, I've created a two-way link. I don't have to put that daily note into a specific project folder. Months later, when I open the Project Alpha API Specs note to review it, the Linked Mentions panel on the right side of my screen automatically shows me that exact sentence from my daily journal.

The system organizes itself based on context, not rigid hierarchies. It mirrors how your actual brain works—forming connections between related concepts naturally over time.

Step 4: Automating Dashboards with Dataview

When your vault is small, finding things is easy. But when your vault hits a thousand notes, manually finding which projects are active and which are stalled becomes incredibly painful. I use the Dataview plugin to build automated, self-updating dashboards so I never have to maintain manual index pages.

On my primary home dashboard (the page that opens every time I launch Obsidian), I use a specific query to automatically list every active project I'm currently working on. It looks like this: 

```dataview
TABLE status AS "Status", deadline AS "Deadline"
FROM #project
WHERE status != "completed"
SORT deadline ASC
```

This small block of code is incredibly powerful. It instantly scans my entire vault for any note containing the #project tag. It filters out any projects where I've set the status to "completed" in the metadata, and then it sorts the remaining active projects by their deadline. It's a live, zero-maintenance dashboard. When I finish a project and change its status, it automatically disappears from this list. I don't have to delete anything or move files around manually.

Troubleshooting & Common Edge Cases

Building a Second Brain isn't without friction. It took me months of tweaking to get this right. Here are the things I screwed up early on, and how you can avoid making the same mistakes:

  • The "Over-Tagging" Trap: When I first started using Obsidian, I tagged every single note with ten different tags. A note about a router config would have `#networking`, `#homelab`, `#cisco`, `#hardware`, and `#reference`. It made my tag index completely useless because everything was tagged with everything. Stick to broad, actionable tags like #project, #meeting, or #reference. Rely on backlinks for detailed connections between topics.
  • Broken Dataview Queries: If your Dataview dashboard suddenly shows zero results, don't panic. You likely have a typo in your frontmatter (metadata) or you misspelled the tag in the query. YAML frontmatter at the top of your files is very strict about spacing. Ensure you have a space after the colon (e.g., `status: active`, not `status:active`).
  • Performance Slowdowns: Obsidian is incredibly fast because it's just rendering local text files. However, if you embed hundreds of massive, high-resolution images or PDFs directly into a single dashboard note, it will start to stutter as it loads them all into memory. Store large assets externally, or link to them rather than embedding them all on one highly-trafficked dashboard page.
  • Mobile Sync Frustrations: If you use the official Obsidian Sync, it's seamless. But if you try to use iCloud to sync between a Windows PC and an iPhone, you will eventually run into duplicated files and sync conflicts. If you are serious about this setup, either pay for Obsidian Sync or set up a proper Git-based sync workflow to avoid pulling your hair out.

Conclusion & Next Steps for Your Vault

A Second Brain isn't about perfectly organizing data or having the most beautiful folder structure; it's about offloading the mental burden so you have the cognitive bandwidth to actually execute on your ideas.

If you take nothing else away from this guide, remember this: start small. Lean heavily on your daily notes, link concepts together using brackets instead of worrying about folders, and let the system evolve organically based on how you actually work. Don't try to build a massive, complex architecture on day one.
