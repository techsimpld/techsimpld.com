Obsidian Tutorial

3 Settings You MUST Change in Obsidian

TS
By Tech Simpld
•
Available Now
•
6 Min Read

Obsidian is hyped relentlessly across YouTube and Reddit as the ultimate "second brain" tool that will magically solve all of your productivity problems. But let's be completely honest with each other: your first ten minutes with the app are usually awful. You download it, excitedly create a vault, and are instantly greeted by a blank, sterile screen that does not behave like any normal word processor you have ever used in your entire life. Formatting breaks unexpectedly, images you paste dump into random places, and the famous Graph View that everyone raves about looks like a tangled mess of useless dots.

I nearly uninstalled Obsidian on my very first day using it. I just wanted to write a simple, structured technical guide for my Proxmox lab, but I spent 30 minutes fighting the editor's default behaviors. It felt like I had to learn a completely new paradigm just to type a grocery list.

The good news? You do not need to learn complex Markdown formatting or how to write custom CSS to fix this. In this specific guide, I am going to walk you through the exact three core settings you must change immediately upon installing the app. These specific tweaks will force Obsidian to behave predictably, keep your file directory pristine and organized, and make the Graph View actually useful for finding data rather than just being eye candy. Let's fix your vault.

Step 1: Fix Paragraph Spacing (Strict Line Breaks)

Because Obsidian relies entirely on standard Markdown syntax beneath the hood, it handles the "Enter" key much differently than a traditional word processor like Microsoft Word or Google Docs. If you type a sentence, hit Enter once, and type a second sentence, it looks perfectly fine while you are in Editing Mode. But the absolute second you switch to Reading Mode, Obsidian smashes those two lines together into a single, unreadable block of text. Standard Markdown strictly requires you to hit Enter twice to create a new paragraph.

This is incredibly frustrating when you are just trying to write a quick bulleted list or a rapid-fire bash script for a server deployment. Nobody wants to consciously retrain their muscle memory just to hit the Enter key twice. It breaks your train of thought completely.

The Fix:

  • Click the gear icon in the bottom left corner of the application to open the main Settings menu.
  • Click on the Editor tab located in the left sidebar.
  • Scroll down through the options until you find the toggle for Strict line breaks.
  • Toggle this setting completely Off.

That is it. Now, hitting the Enter key just once actually creates a proper new line when you switch to Reading Mode. It instantly makes the editor feel like a normal, friendly word processor and removes the biggest friction point for new users.

Step 2: Stop Image Clutter (The Attachment Folder Rule)

When you paste a quick screenshot into Apple Notes or Evernote, the application handles the file management invisibly in the background. It embeds seamlessly. However, in Obsidian, because everything you do is just a local file on your hard drive, pasting an image creates an actual, physical .png file inside your vault directory.

By default, Obsidian is configured to dump every single pasted image directly into your root vault folder. If you take visual notes, or if you are documenting a software deployment with a lot of UI screenshots, your sidebar will quickly become an unreadable garbage dump of files named Pasted image 20261012.png. Finding your actual, written markdown notes becomes absolutely impossible because they are buried under hundreds of image files.

The Fix:

We need to explicitly force Obsidian to hide all of these media files in a dedicated background folder so you never have to look at them.

  • In your main file explorer sidebar within Obsidian, create a brand new folder. I highly recommend naming it Attachments or System Assets.
  • Open the Settings menu again and click on the Files and links tab.
  • Find the setting labeled Default location for new attachments.
  • Change the dropdown menu from the default "Vault folder" to the option that says In the folder specified below.
  • In the new text box that appears directly underneath it, type exactly what you named your folder (e.g., Attachments) and select it from the auto-complete list.

From this point forward, every single screenshot, PDF document, or audio file you drag and drop into a note will be silently routed to that background folder. Your root directory stays perfectly clean, showing only the text files you actually care about.

Step 3: Make the Graph View Actually Usable

The Graph View is Obsidian's most heavily marketed feature. It looks incredibly cool in promotional videos. But as your vault grows past a hundred notes, the default settings turn it into a dense, unreadable ball of overlapping gray dots sitting dead in the center of your screen. It looks neat, but it is functionally useless for actually navigating your knowledge base or finding lost information.

We need to apply specific physics forces to spread those nodes apart, and we need to add color-coding so you can instantly recognize what topics you are looking at without having to hover over every single dot.

The Fix:

  • Open the Graph View and click the small gear icon located in its top right corner. This will slide out the Graph settings panel.
  • Expand the Forces tab. You will see several sliders here. Grab the Repulsion slider and drag it significantly to the right (around 75% or higher). This acts exactly like a magnet, pushing related notes away from each other and instantly untangling that massive, dense clump of dots in the middle.
  • Next, expand the Groups tab and click the button for a New group.
  • In the search field for the new group, type path:Projects (or whatever the name of your active projects folder is). Click the color swatch next to it and select a bright, highly visible color like Blue or Cyan.
  • Create another new group, type tag:#meeting, and select a distinct color like Green.

Watch what happens. Your graph will immediately expand into a clear, readable constellation, beautifully color-coded by context. You can now actually use the graph to spot orphaned notes that aren't linked to anything, or visually see how dense and complex a specific project is getting over time. It transforms the graph from a gimmick into a genuine navigation tool.

Troubleshooting & Edge Cases

If things still aren't working exactly right after making these tweaks, double-check these common pitfalls:

  • My Graph View is still completely empty: The graph only draws lines and connects notes if you actually create links. If you just type plain text and never use the double-bracket [[Wikilink]] syntax to connect your thoughts, the graph will just be isolated, floating dots that never touch. You must actively link your notes together to form those connections.
  • Images are still going to the root folder: Double-check that you actually spelled your attachment folder correctly in the settings menu, and verify you didn't accidentally select the "In subfolder under current folder" option by mistake. Obsidian is very literal about paths.
  • Reading mode still looks weird with line breaks: If you are using a very heavy custom CSS theme from the community store, it might forcefully override the Strict Line Breaks setting we just changed. Try reverting to the default Obsidian theme temporarily to isolate the issue and see if the theme is the culprit.

Conclusion & Next Steps for Your Vault

Obsidian is a phenomenally powerful engine, but it unfortunately ships with terrible factory defaults that scare away a lot of potential users. By simply turning off strict line breaks, routing your attachments to a dedicated hidden folder, and tuning the graph physics, you remove 90% of the friction that new users experience.

Your vault is now clean, predictable, and ready for actual, serious work. Instead of fighting the application, you can finally focus on writing great documentation, tracking your homelab projects, or building out your second brain.
